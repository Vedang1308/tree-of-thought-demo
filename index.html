Tree of Thoughts

Deliberate problem solving with large language models

Shunyu Yao, Dian Yu, Jeffrey Zhao, Izhak Shafran, Thomas L. Griffiths, Yuan Cao, Karthik Narasimhan
Princeton University · Google DeepMind

A language model writes one token at a time, left to right, and never takes a token back. That is fine for many tasks. It breaks when an early choice decides everything and the solver needs to try options, judge them, and back up.

Tree of Thoughts (ToT) puts the same model inside a search. The model proposes several next steps, rates its own partial solutions, and the search keeps exploring only the promising ones. No training is needed.

    System 1

    Fast and associative. By default an LM picks each next token from what came before and never revisits a choice.

    System 2

    Slow and deliberate. Keep several options open, judge them, look ahead, back up. ToT adds this layer around the same LM.

    Roots

    Newell, Shaw & Simon (1950s) described problem solving as search through a tree of partial solutions, steered by heuristics. In ToT the LM itself supplies the heuristic.

    Background · paper §2 and Figure 1

    Four ways to get an answer out of an LM

    Each box is a thought: a short piece of text that is one step toward the answer, such as one equation. Press → to walk through them one at a time.

    Input–output

    y ∼ pθ(y | x)

    pθ is the language model (θ are its weights). It reads the input x and writes the answer y in one go.

    Missing: any room to work things out.

    Chain of thought

    z1 … zn, y ∼ pθ(· | x)

    The model writes intermediate thoughts z1…zn, then the answer, in a single left-to-right pass.

    Missing: a second path. A bad first step can't be undone.

    Self-consistency

    ŷ = argmaxy #{ i : y(i) = y }

    Run chain of thought k times independently and return the answer that comes up most often (# counts the votes).

    Missing: branching inside a chain. Voting also needs a small answer space.

    Tree of thoughts

    s = [x, z1…i] · G(pθ, s, k) · V(pθ, S)

    Each node s is a partial solution: the input plus the thoughts so far. G asks the LM for k next thoughts; V asks the LM to rate states; BFS or DFS picks what to expand.

    Adds: branching, self-evaluation, lookahead and backtracking.

    Gap 1 · local

    Left-to-right methods never explore different continuations of a thought process. There are no branches.

    Gap 2 · global

    They don't plan, look ahead, or backtrack to compare options, which is how people search a problem space.

    ToT contains the others

    IO, CoT, CoT-SC and self-refinement are special cases: trees with limited depth and breadth.

    Method · paper §3, Algorithms 1–2

    Every ToT answers four design questions

    The framework is modular. You choose what counts as a thought, how to generate them, how to judge them, and how to search. The LM is never fine-tuned.

      Experiment 2 · paper §4.2 and Figure 4

      Creative writing: sample plans, then vote

      There is no equation to check, so a plan is hard to score on its own. Instead the LM compares candidates side by side and votes. The tree is two levels deep and keeps one choice per level (b = 1).

      Input

      Write a coherent passage of 4 short paragraphs. The last sentence of each paragraph must be:

      1. The lighthouse keeper had never once seen a ship.
      2. Nobody expected the parrot to know the password.
      3. By noon, the whole bakery smelled of burnt sugar.
      4. Every map is a list of things someone decided to leave out.
      depth 2 · k = 5 samples · 5 votes · b = 1zero-shot vote prompt: "analyze the choices, then say which is most promising"

        Experiment 3 · paper §4.3 and Figure 6

        Mini crosswords: go deep, prune, backtrack

        A 5×5 grid has 10 words that constrain each other, so the search is deeper (5–10 steps). ToT fills one word per step with depth-first search and backs up when the LM decides a remaining clue can no longer be filled.

          Discussion · paper §6

          Takeaways

          An LM can be both the move generator and the heuristic inside a classical search, and that alone turns 4% into 74% on a planning puzzle.

          What ToT offers

          • General. IO, CoT, CoT-SC and self-refine are trees of limited depth and breadth.
          • Modular. The LM, thought size, generator, evaluator and search can each be swapped.
          • Adaptable. Depth, breadth and number of samples trade cost for accuracy.
          • No training. A pre-trained LM and a few prompts are enough.
          • Readable. Every decision is written in plain language, which helps interpretability.

          Limits the paper names

          • Cost. Many more LM calls than CoT: 5–100× the tokens in these experiments.
          • Often unnecessary. On tasks GPT-4 already handles (GSM8K), the gain is small.
          • Imperfect heuristics. The evaluator pruned correct crossword answers it didn't recognize.
          • Simple search. Only BFS and DFS; A* and MCTS are left for future work.
          • Off-the-shelf LMs. Fine-tuning on ToT-style decisions is an open direction.

          Questions for discussion

          • When is self-evaluation reliable enough to prune on? What happens when the LM is confidently wrong?
          • Which tasks you care about have an early decision that is hard to undo?
          • Could a model learn to search like this internally, instead of through an external loop?
          • How would you pick the thought size for a new task, such as coding or data analysis?
